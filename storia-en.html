Ancient Roots of Tuna

The oldest traces of tuna fishing

Tuna fishing in the Mediterranean has millenary origins: the graffiti in the Genovese Cave on Levanzo tell of a hunting practice already in use between 10,000 and 6,000 BC.

Phoenicians and Greeks considered tuna a strategic commodity. Strabo describes it as a precious good transported as far as Cadiz, while Aristotle observes its migratory route from the Strait of Gibraltar into the inner seas.

Tuna was not only fishing: it was a reason for exploration and trade, so much so that coins bearing the fish were minted in the Atlantic. Its history crosses Phoenicians, Greeks, Romans, Byzantines, Arabs and Normans, up to the modern Salento tuna fishery era.

Homer, Polybius, Pliny, Plutarch and Galen also wrote about tuna, but it is thanks to Archestratus of Gela, the first gourmet poet, that we have the earliest descriptions of durable preservation methods. Martial praises it as a food in itself, a hearty meal for travelers who love strong flavors.

The almadraba and the net labyrinths

The almadraba is an ancient fishing technique, widespread in Italy, Morocco, Portugal and Spain. Derived from Andalusian Arabic almaḍrába, it means 'a place where one strikes'.

Phoenicians

They sailed the Mediterranean and considered tuna a precious commodity for voyages beyond the Pillars of Hercules.

Ancient Greece

Archestratus of Gela describes long-lasting preservation methods and the use of tuna in ancient cuisine.

Rome

Martial celebrates tuna in his elegant banquet recipes and ties its consumption to aristocratic taste.

Culture and Rituals

The Cialoma Song

The work song, or cialoma, sung by the tonnaroti as they slowly and rhythmically hoisted the heavy net curtain, is an ethnographic treasure. They invoke Jesus Christ, all the saints, and also Allah, because the first words “Aja mola” are a Sicilian deformation of “Ai ya mawla”, Arabic for “O my Lord.”

The tonnara, with its nets, anchors, boats, craftsmen, fishermen and families, carries the imprint of all the civilizations that used it: Phoenician, Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Norman and later the modern post-Renaissance era.

Oral Tradition

The song marked the rhythm of the work and reinforced the collective identity of the tonnaroti.

Words and Faith

Prayer, devotion and religious references mixed Christianity with Arabic heritage.

Archive and document

From the Red Book to the lost documents

The transfer of the entire ancient municipal archive to the then provincial archive repository is recorded in the 1833 report written by municipal clerk Carlo Mazzarella and signed by mayor Francesco Pasca.

Even the city’s pre-unification documents were once stored in the Garibaldi theatre and were sold to a junk dealer, a process that led to legal proceedings and a long story of dispersal.

Source: Gallipoli_Archivio_delle_scritture_antic.pdf

These documents are not just a bureaucratic list: they represent the memory of a community that governed the sea, the nets and the rules of the tonnara.

Sea Lexicon & Dialect

The Tuna Fishery Glossary

The words of the tonnarotti: a linguistic heritage born from the meeting of Arabic, Latin, Greek and the Gallipoli dialect.

Rais Dialetto: Lu 'Rrais

From Arabic ra'īs (supreme chief)

The absolute commander of the tuna fishery. He had total authority over the manoeuvres, the lowering of the nets, the discipline of the tonnarotti and the exact hour of the mattanza.

Cialoma Dialetto: La Cialuma / Lu Canti

From Arabic jalā (invocation/song)

The rhythmic chant sung by the crew to coordinate, with bare arms, the recovery and hauling of the extremely heavy wet nets made of vegetable fibre.

Pedale Dialetto: Lu Pedali / Cavo da Terra

From Latin pedalis (base anchorage)

The long wall of net that ran directly from the coastal cliff (between Punta Pizzo and the gulf) and stretched into the sea to block the tuna's path.

Muciara Dialetto: La Muciara / La Varchicedda

From Arabic mugiarah (light boat)

The smallest and fastest rowing boat, boarded exclusively by the Rais to inspect the nets, spot the arrival of the shoals and give orders.

Camera della Morte Dialetto: Lu Corpu / La Cammera

The last basin of the fixed plant

The only net basin fitted with a bottom (called lu corpu). Once the passage was closed, the net was hauled up from the vessels for the final phase of the catch.

Tonno Rosso Dialetto: Lu Tùnnuru / Lu Pisce Rrussu

Thunnus thynnus

The majestic Mediterranean pelagics that, in spring, travelled up the warm currents of the Ionian Sea, passing off the waters of Gallipoli.

Historical Feature • Pre-Roman

The Japigi and the Messapian Origin of the Tuna Fishery

In his historical works, the Gallipoli patrician Filippo Briganti (18th century) states that tuna fishing in Salento has pre-Roman roots. The Japigi, and especially the Messapi, had noticed the strong schooling behavior of tuna gathering near the coast in spring to spawn.

With rudimentary methods, the Messapi built the first fixed tuna traps: wooden palisades and funnel-shaped nets along the inlets, where tuna were later captured by hand using large hooks or long spears.

"Dunque le prime tonnare pugliesi, cioè japigie, furono a segno messapico..." — Filippo Briganti
Historical Feature • 15th - 16th Cent.

The "Red Book" and Royal Privileges

Piri Reis, Map of the coast of Gallipoli and Nardò (16th century) — public domain

During Aragonese and Spanish rule, the operation of the tuna fishery was officially regulated by royal authorities. The Red Book of the University (Municipality) of Gallipoli records port duty concessions and the monopoly on jurisdictional waters, including the diploma of Charles V in 1526.

The management of the facility provided work for hundreds of tuna fishermen and carpenters, supporting a rich salted tuna trade throughout the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies.

Historical Feature • 18th Century

The Giant Funnel and the Pizzo Tuna Fishery

Jean-Pierre Houël, Tuna fishing, etching (1782) — public domain

In the 18th century, the Gallipoli tuna fishery reached its largest industrial expansion. As documented by Briganti, its network funnel was so immense (about 1,600 meters of barriers) that even whales and monk seals sometimes became entangled in its meshes.

The main facility in the gulf was coordinated with the secondary Pizzo tuna fishery to the south, forming a perfect maritime defensive system.

Historical Feature • 1960s - 70s

The Last Slaughter and Closure

During the 20th century, the arrival of industrial fishing steamers and changing underwater migration routes gradually depleted the Ionian waters.

In the 1970s, the fixed Gallipoli tuna fishery lowered its nets for the final time, turning the age-old fishing ritual into an invaluable intangible heritage.
